# Independent Engineering Practice — Launch Plan

**Cycle:** November 7–December 6, 2026  
**Decision date:** December 6  
**Possible next cycle:** December 7–20  
**Capacity:** 8 hours per week planned; up to 2 extra hours only when classes and work allow.

## Outcome

Build a portfolio that lets a prospective client understand what I can deliver, inspect evidence, and contact me in under two minutes. Use a small content launch and relevant personal conversations to test one engineering service offer.

By December 6, I want a working inquiry-to-quotation process and evidence about what people need. Revenue is a desired outcome, not a promise. This cycle uses existing projects; building a new robot or redesigning the entire portfolio is outside its scope.

## 1. Success criteria

Separate work I control from market responses I can only influence.

| Type                  | Target by December 6                                                             | Evidence                                                         |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Delivery              | Work With Me section live, two case studies, one primary contact route           | Working URLs; desktop and phone checks                           |
| Delivery              | Six short videos published                                                       | Publication links; four is the reduced-capacity fallback         |
| Delivery              | Inquiry, proposal, invoice, and handover templates ready                         | Reusable drafts with no invented client details                  |
| Learning              | Three short portfolio feedback sessions                                          | Notes from people resembling the intended audience               |
| Learning              | Six relevant, individual conversations or sharing opportunities across the cycle | Source, response, and useful questions logged; no bulk messaging |
| Demand hypothesis     | Three genuine inquiries                                                          | Unique people asking about a specific project or problem         |
| Commercial hypothesis | One suitable quotation sent                                                      | Written scope, acceptance test, timeline, and price              |
| Stretch               | One paid pilot accepted                                                          | Written agreement and initial payment received                   |

A compliment, follower, or vague “can you help?” message is not yet a genuine inquiry. A **qualified inquiry** also has a plausible fit, an identified decision-maker, and enough information about access, timing, and budget to assess the work. Count people once; record repeat conversations under the same inquiry.

Do not send an unsuitable quotation merely to hit the target. If there is no suitable lead, complete a clearly labeled sample proposal and record the demand target as unmet.

## 3. Capacity and priorities

A normal week has two three-hour work blocks, one hour for replies/tracking, and one hour of buffer. Choose the actual days around classes; calendar booking is a next action, not already completed.

**Priority order:** credible proof → clear offer and working contact → conversations → videos → cosmetic polish.

| Week | Planned work | Hours |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Nov 9–15 | Offer/contact page 2; two concise case studies 4; testing and basic inquiry log 1; buffer 1 | 8 |
| Nov 16–22 | Two videos and reusable edit template 3; inquiry/proposal/invoice/handover drafts 2; portfolio feedback and distribution 1; replies/tracking 1; buffer 1 | 8 |
| Nov 23–29 | Two videos 3; lead conversations or scoping 2; one portfolio improvement 1; replies/tracking 1; buffer 1 | 8 |
| Nov 30–Dec 6 | Two videos 3; lead follow-up or quotation 1; results review and next-cycle decision 2; replies/tracking 1; buffer 1 | 8 |

The one-hour November 7–8 preparation brings the baseline to **33 hours**. Optional hours are for overruns, missing proof, or a real inquiry—not automatic scope expansion.

### Reduce scope when needed

- **6–7 hours available:** publish one video instead of two that week; keep portfolio proof and inquiry handling.
- **Under 6 hours available:** pause video production, complete the smallest useful portfolio milestone, and respond to existing leads.
- **Paid work arrives:** replace planned content time with delivery time. Do not add the client project on top of a full schedule.
- **A task exceeds its estimate by an hour:** stop and reduce its scope or swap out another task before continuing.
- **Exams or work intensify:** publish four videos across the cycle, or fewer if necessary. Record the capacity change rather than treating it as a marketing failure.

## 5. Portfolio requirements

### Work With Me outline

1. **Who I help and what I do:** one plain sentence naming the audience and a concrete integration outcome.
2. **Lead offer:** a small, bounded task; list typical inputs, deliverables, and how acceptance is tested.
3. **Proof:** link directly to the two relevant case studies and demonstrations.
4. **Other capabilities:** a short secondary list, limited to work I can deliver confidently.
5. **Process:** inquiry → assessment → scoped proposal → implementation → demonstration and handover.
6. **Fit and limits:** prototype work, supported environments, availability, and exclusions.
7. **Contact:** one prominent action, expected reply time, and the five inquiry questions.

**Draft lead-offer wording to adapt after selecting the offer:**

> I help small robotics teams connect and troubleshoot ROS 2 components on an existing prototype. A small engagement starts with one defined integration problem and ends with a tested change, setup notes, and a demonstration against agreed acceptance criteria.

Avoid promising a complete autonomous system when the deliverable is one integration task. Use “Robotics & Embedded Software” as a working identity; make specific capability claims only where the case studies support them.

### Case-study structure

Keep the original eight sections, but make each earn its place:

| Section | What the reader should learn |
| --- | --- |
| Overview | What works, who it is for, and a short demonstration |
| Problem | The actual requirement and constraints |
| Architecture | Main components, interfaces, and data/control flow |
| Implementation | Two or three significant engineering choices |
| My contribution | Exactly what I built, integrated, tested, or debugged; credit collaborators |
| Demonstration | Footage, screenshots, logs, or reproducible steps |
| Results | Observed outcomes with the test conditions and sample size where relevant |
| Limitations | Failure cases, remaining work, and prototype boundaries |

Do not invent performance numbers. If a result is qualitative, label it as an observation. Distinguish simulation, bench testing, and hardware testing.

### Two-minute portfolio check

Ask someone to open the site on their own device and answer:

- What can this person help you build or fix?
- Which example makes that claim credible?
- What would you send them to start a conversation?

Record their answers before explaining the site. Check contact-link behavior, readability, navigation, image loading, and whether demonstrations work without special access. Fix the largest obstacle first.

## 7. Offer, pricing, and client workflow

### Scope the first engagement tightly

Start with a bounded pilot: one device interface, one ROS 2 component integration, one communication path, or one reproducible failure. If the unknowns prevent a reliable implementation estimate, propose a separate diagnostic stage with a concrete output, such as a reproduction, findings, and a recommended next step.

Before quoting, establish:

- [ ] The problem, desired outcome, and one observable acceptance test.
- [ ] Hardware/software versions, repository access, and available test equipment.
- [ ] What the client provides and when it will be available.
- [ ] Deadline, decision-maker, and budget range or constraint.
- [ ] Deliverables, exclusions, dependencies, and revision limit.
- [ ] My realistic delivery capacity alongside classes and work.

### Pricing method

Treat all prices as **internal experiments**, not verified market rates. The original $10–15 consultation, $30–60 firmware, $50–120 ROS 2, and $80–180 IoT figures are unvalidated hypotheses. Do not publish them as commitments before estimating a real scope.

Use this worksheet for each quotation:

| Input | Estimate |
| --- | --- |
| Discovery / reproduction | ___ hours |
| Implementation | ___ hours |
| Testing on the agreed setup | ___ hours |
| Documentation and handover | ___ hours |
| Communication and included revisions | ___ hours |
| Explicit allowance for identified uncertainty | ___ hours |
| Total estimated effort | ___ hours |
| Minimum acceptable hourly return, chosen internally | $___ |
| Direct costs and known payment fees | $___ |
| Proposed fixed quote and currency | ___ |

A starting calculation is **estimated effort × internal hourly floor + direct costs and known fees**. Explain assumptions; revise the scope or offer diagnosis if uncertainty is too large for a fixed quote. After delivery, compare the quote with actual hours before changing prices.

Keep a brief fit conversation free if desired, but give it a time limit. Technical investigation is a separate deliverable. Discuss discounts by reducing scope, not by quietly promising the same work for less.

### Terms to settle before accepting work

These are draft operating choices to discuss with the client, not finalized contractual or jurisdiction-specific advice.

- Written scope, deliverables, acceptance criteria, and an agreed schedule.
- A defined revision limit; two rounds is a starting option. New features require a new estimate.
- A proposed 30–50% initial payment for custom work, or another explicitly agreed structure; define milestones, cancellation handling, and remaining payment timing.
- A support window for defects against the agreed scope; maintenance and future features priced separately.
- Clear prototype limitations, client dependencies, and handover expectations.
- Agreement about code/assets, third-party licenses, confidentiality, and permission to show any client work publicly.
- A usable payment method, currency, invoice details, and responsibility for payment fees; confirm applicable local requirements before taking payment.

Do not offer complete industrial automation, advanced PCB design, production-grade AI, open-ended debugging, or safety-critical deployment unless capability and scope have been established separately. Keep one active pilot in this cycle.

### Copy-ready client templates

**Inquiry**

> 1. What are you building, and who will use it?  
> 2. What hardware, software, and versions are involved?  
> 3. What specifically needs to be built or fixed, and what would success look like?  
> 4. What is your deadline, and is it flexible?  
> 5. What budget range or spending constraint should I work within?

**First reply**

> Thanks for the details. My understanding is [problem and desired outcome]. Before suggesting a scope, I need [missing information]. I can respond with [a scoped proposal / a diagnostic option / a referral or decline] by [realistic date]. Please share a non-confidential example first; we can agree on access if the project proceeds.

**One-page proposal fields**

> Client and date · problem · scope · deliverables · acceptance test · exclusions · client inputs · milestones and timeline · price and currency · payments · revisions · support · code/assets and permissions · cancellation terms · quote validity · written acceptance.

**Invoice draft fields**

> Unique reference · issue date · sender and client details · project/milestone reference · description · amount and currency · any applicable tax/fee treatment · due date · payment instructions · payment status. Confirm required fields for the actual business/payment setup before issuing.

**Handover note**

> Delivered files and versions · setup and run instructions · acceptance-test results · known limitations · remaining client actions · support end date · agreed handling of access credentials · final payment status.

## 9. December 6 decision

Change one major thing at a time so the next cycle can teach something useful.

| Evidence | Next action |
| --- | --- |
| Several qualified inquiries cluster around one problem | Make that problem the lead offer; use its language in the portfolio and next two clips |
| People watch but rarely visit the portfolio | Check the actual profile/contact path and strengthen the connection between the demonstration and offer |
| People visit but do not inquire | Use feedback to test offer clarity, relevance of proof, and contact effort; do not infer price is the only cause |
| Inquiries arrive but are mostly outside scope | Narrow audience, examples, and qualification questions |
| Suitable inquiries cannot support the effort required | Reduce scope, introduce a paid diagnostic stage, or test a minimum engagement; do not automatically discount |
| A qualified quotation is declined | Record the stated reason before changing price, scope, or terms |
| Little response after six clips and six relevant conversations | Keep the portfolio gains; test the secondary channel and clearer audience/problem fit before adding content volume |
| A paid pilot is accepted | Prioritize delivery and evidence; reduce posting to fit the same weekly capacity |
| The workload disrupts classes, work, or rest | Pause acquisition or run the reduced-capacity version |
